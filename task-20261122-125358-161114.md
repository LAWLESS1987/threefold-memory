AFTER (outbound t262s1112, verbatim):
I worked through this on paper only. I didn't build anything or check the library, and I've marked where I'm unsure.

1. **The key derivation.** Sound if either public-key scheme breaks. The key depends on both s1 and s2, and putting sha256(ek) and sha256(kc) in `info` binds the ciphertexts, which is what makes a concatenation combiner safe. Order, fixed lengths and the zero salt are fine. Having the header in both `info` and the additional data is redundant but harmless. One note: ML-KEM decapsulation never visibly fails (it returns a pseudorandom key on a bad `kc`), so its failure shows up as a GCM tag failure. That's good for rule 3, but it means "both unwraps" really means one unwrap plus a tag check.

2. **Binding.** Re-addressing, replaying to another recipient, stripping `kc` and swapping the sender all fail. `tof`, `tok` and `fromf` sit in the header, which goes into the key, so a re-signed block derives a wrong key. The gap is replay to the same recipient. The nonce and day rules cover decisions only, so an old plan block, signed and valid, would be accepted again unless the plan also carries a day or sequence number the reader checks. I'm not sure whether plans already do.

3. **The downgrade rule.** The reader's own ledger is the right place, but it has three problems.
   - If it's a date, it has to be compared against the reader's clock, never the block's day, which a key-breaker can write.
   - A switch ("v2 required from now") is simpler than a future date.
   - There's no sender-side rule. The sender should refuse to produce v1 once the peer has announced an ML-KEM key, or the owner's own device keeps creating harvestable v1 mail until the reader's date arrives.

4. **The key announcement.** It's enough only because it's checked now, while RSA still holds, and then pinned. An attacker with a quantum computer at announcement time forges an announcement with their own ML-KEM key, learns s1 and s2, and opens every v2 block, so v2 gains nothing. After pinning, they gain nothing, unless a later rotation signed only by RSA is accepted, which hands them the same win. The statement's own time is RSA-signed and could be backdated later, so the ledger's acceptance date is what counts. Fix: compare `tok` with the devices side by side when the key is first pinned, and never accept an ML-KEM rotation signed by RSA alone.

5. **The shared secret.** Yes, it's a real third protection, and it does more than the packet claims. With s3 unknown, nobody can derive the content key, so GCM also authenticates the block against an attacker who has broken both public-key schemes. Until ML-DSA arrives, it's the only post-quantum authenticity in this design. Hole: `"psk": false` is accepted by a reader that has a PSK for that sender, so a key-breaker just sends blocks without the PSK. The reader needs a "PSK required" entry per sender in the ledger, the same as rule 4. Renewal should happen in person only, never over the network, with the new name pinned in the ledger. Old blocks stay readable only as long as the old secret is kept. Deleting it after the plaintexts are archived locally makes the mail providers' copies permanently safe, and keeping it makes them only as safe as the device that holds it.

6. **Version 1's habits.**
   - Canonical JSON should be one stated standard (for example RFC 8785), because the signature and the additional data both depend on byte-identical serialisation, and floats and escapes are where implementations differ.
   - The carried `spk` should go. Look the key up by `fromf` instead, so the reader never parses a key the attacker supplied.
   - The 16-hex fingerprints are fine for display, but nothing should ever look keys up or authorise anything by them.
   - The PSS salt length should be fixed at 32 bytes and checked, not taken from the encoded message.
   - The order of checks is right. Do the cheap checks (`v`, `suite`, `tof`) before the signature, and keep the day and nonce checks after decryption.

7. **The unknown adversary.** With unlimited computing power, AES, SHA-256 and HKDF fall too, so the v2 PSK doesn't help either. The most the owner can still protect is the content and integrity of messages up to the size of a pad exchanged in person. The least that needs is a truly random one-time pad carried between the devices physically, every byte used once and then destroyed, plus a one-time MAC key per message (Wegman-Carter style), and devices that aren't themselves compromised. At a few KB a day, a 1 GB pad lasts hundreds of years. Nothing protects the metadata: that a message exists, its size and its timing are all visible to the mail providers.

**Most important change:** do the setup in person and pin it. Compare `tok` and exchange the PSK at one meeting, then refuse `psk: false`, v1, and any RSA-only key rotation from that sender after that.
